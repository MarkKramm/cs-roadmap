# CS Roadmap Specialisation, Portfolio, Certification and Search — curriculum practice exam

**Unofficial curriculum practice exam; not a certification exam or a job-readiness guarantee.** This diagnostic covers the four cyber phases that turn study into a candidacy: choosing a specialisation and committing to it, building projects a stranger can check, deciding what a certification is worth to you specifically, and running a job search without misreading silence as failure. It is free practice and does not certify readiness for any role.

## How to use and score it

Answer all questions before checking the marked answers. Give yourself one point per correct answer, then read every explanation. **There is no pass/fail score or readiness threshold.** Use missed or guessed questions to identify which phase to revisit.

**These four phases mostly do not test knowledge, and these questions do not either.** They test reasoning about your own situation: whether you counted something or trusted a reputation, whether you scored a constraint as a veto or averaged it away, whether you can say what your own evidence does and does not prove. Almost every wrong answer here is a plausible mistake a careful person makes rather than a fact nobody knows.

**A note on what is a fact and what is a position.** Several of the strongest claims in these phases are the curriculum's reasoning rather than established truth — that a certification moves you up a candidate pile without moving you into a different one, that the resume screen is a filter rather than a judge, that a decision to delay a certification is a *successful* completion of that phase. Where a question turns on one of those, the stem asks **what the phase says**, and the correct answer is the phase's stated position.

**Three numeric thresholds appear across these four phases and they are not interchangeable:** Phase 5 asks for **20** counted job postings, Phase 7 for **30** postings in its certification test, and Phase 8 for **50** targeted applications. The phase is named in every question that uses one, because mixing them up is a coin flip rather than a mistake about judgement.

**A note on scope, since it is the most common way to read these phases wrongly.** No question here asks about salary as a decision criterion, because Phase 5 addresses salary only as an expectation to calibrate and never as a basis for choosing. No question asks what these phases say about cover letters or about following up on applications, because **neither phase takes a position to test**: Phase 8 mentions cover letters exactly once, as a distractor in its own quiz rather than as advice, and says almost nothing about follow-up beyond a tracker column and a date field. Those are real gaps in the curriculum, not oversights in the exam, and inventing a position to test would be worse than leaving it untested.

## Domain 1 — Choosing and committing (5 questions)

### Q1. Phase 5's exit criterion requires a statement of the form "My target role is ___ and my portfolio will prove it using these 3 projects: ___." A learner can name the role but not the three projects. What does the phase say about their position?
<!-- phases: cyber-05-specialization-choice -->

- [ ] They have finished the phase, because the role decision is the hard part and the projects follow
- [x] **They have expressed a preference, not finished the phase — the decision and its evidence are one statement**
- [ ] They should return to Phase 4 and complete more lab work before choosing a role
- [ ] They have finished the phase provided they write the three projects down later, once they exist

**Why:** The phase is explicit that the exit criterion is different in kind from every previous phase because it is not a statement about knowledge but a decision plus a plan. Its line is that a learner who can name a target role but not the three projects that prove it has "merely expressed a preference." This is why the third and fourth options are wrong rather than merely weaker: the projects are not a downstream consequence of the decision, they are the evidence that the decision is real and buildable.

### Q2. When scoring the four paths for job availability, the phase says to count real postings rather than trust reputation. What does it say to exclude from the count?
<!-- phases: cyber-05-specialization-choice -->

- [ ] Postings at companies whose names the learner recognises
- [x] **Senior roles, roles requiring a degree the learner does not have, and roles in countries they cannot work in**
- [ ] Postings that mention a certification the learner has not earned
- [ ] Postings older than thirty days, which are assumed to be filled

**Why:** The three exclusions are stated directly, and they all share a property: each describes a posting the learner could not be hired into, so counting it inflates the apparent market. The phase's reason for the exercise is that "learners are consistently surprised by which path has real local junior-level demand, versus which merely has a loud online community" — the count corrects a reputation that is usually derived from visibility rather than vacancies. Note the exclusion is about *eligibility*, not about the posting's quality or its certification wording.

### Q3. A path scores 5 for interest and 2 for difficulty of entry; another scores 3 and 3. Both average 3.5. What does the phase say to do with these scores?
<!-- phases: cyber-05-specialization-choice -->

- [ ] Prefer the second, because difficulty of entry is the axis that can veto a path
- [ ] Treat them as equivalent, since the weighted totals are what decide
- [ ] Prefer the first, because interest is the axis that predicts persistence
- [x] **Do not average them at all — the two profiles are different propositions and the weighting must be written down**

**Why:** The phase says plainly not to average the four scores into one number, and gives this exact example: a path scoring 5 on interest and 2 on difficulty is a different proposition from one scoring 3 and 3, even though both average 3.5. Averaging discards the shape of the profile, which is where the decision lives. The third option is the trap that sounds most like the phase — interest does matter — but the phase's position is that the axes are weighted deliberately and **the weighting is written down**, because "saying that explicitly is planning. Ignoring it is wishful thinking."

### Q4. A learner has a health condition that makes night shift work impossible, and the paths they are considering all require it. How does the phase say to treat this constraint?
<!-- phases: cyber-05-specialization-choice -->

- [x] **As a veto, not a low score — a constraint is not something to be averaged away**
- [ ] As a low score on the shift and lifestyle axis, to be offset by higher scores elsewhere
- [ ] As a reason to reconsider the target role family entirely
- [ ] As a detail to revisit once interviews begin, since not every employer runs shifts

**Why:** The phase's distinction is that veto criteria are not low scores: shift work you cannot do, a path with no local postings, and a portfolio you could not build are vetoes. Averaging is exactly what the design of a weighted matrix invites you to do, which is why the phase names the failure. Its worked example of the GRC-leaning learner turns on this: she treated shift work as a veto rather than a low score, and the phase counts that among the four things that made her reasoning good rather than merely thorough.

### Q5. A learner chooses a SOC role as their target and GRC as their backup. What property does the phase say a backup should have?
<!-- phases: cyber-05-specialization-choice -->

- [ ] It should be the path with the lowest difficulty of entry, as insurance against failing to enter the target
- [ ] It should be the path with the highest weighted score that the learner did not choose
- [ ] It should contrast with the target, so the two searches do not compete for the same postings
- [x] **It should be adjacent, because roughly seventy per cent of the portfolio transfers and thirty per cent is rebuilt**

**Why:** The phase's pivot rule is a 70/30 split, and the reason it prescribes an adjacent backup is that adjacency is what makes the split cheap. Paths that produce documents about systems transfer into each other cheaply, while paths requiring a distinct technical demonstration — pentest most of all — are expensive to enter late. The second option is the tempting inversion: contrast sounds like it hedges the bet, but the phase's point is that contrast is precisely what makes the pivot expensive, which is why a pentest backup to a SOC target is a much larger move than a GRC one.

## Domain 2 — Building evidence (6 questions)

### Q6. Phase 6 says "doing the work and proving the work are two different skills." Which of the following does the phase name as a failure of the *proving* skill rather than the work itself?
<!-- phases: cyber-06-portfolio-projects -->

- [x] **A wall of technical narration with no summary, so a reviewer cannot find the point in a minute**
- [ ] Generating the events in your own lab rather than using a platform scenario
- [ ] Choosing a platform lab whose scenario thousands of others have also written up
- [ ] Building what interests you instead of what the role requires

**Why:** The exit criterion names four separate qualifiers, and each is a distinct failure mode: clear, honest, evidence-based, and aligned. A wall of technical narration with no summary is a failure of **clarity** — the phase's line is that a reviewer spends about a minute before deciding, and if they cannot find the point, the point does not exist. The other three options are real tensions the phase discusses and resolves rather than failures: it tells you to obey role alignment over interest, it accepts the trade-off of your own lab because you know the answer in advance, and it offers "platform lab plus going beyond its questions" as the strongest form rather than rejecting platform labs.

### Q7. The phase's 11-section report anatomy includes a Limitations section. Why does it call this the fastest way to sound like an analyst rather than a student?
<!-- phases: cyber-06-portfolio-projects -->

- [ ] Because a limitation gives the reviewer something to ask about in an interview
- [ ] Because a limitation shows the work was difficult and therefore creditable
- [ ] Because omitting one is the most common reason a report is rejected by a screener
- [x] **Because stating what your evidence cannot show demonstrates you know the boundary of your own claim**

**Why:** The model sentence the phase gives is "I confirmed the process tree but could not determine the parent process, because the host was reimaged before I captured volatile data" — which names both the finding and its edge. The reason this reads as analyst rather than student is that an analyst's output is always bounded by their evidence, and volunteering the bound is the evidence that you understand it. The fourth option is a genuine side benefit and not the reason the phase gives, which is the distinction the question is testing.

### Q8. In the phase's weak-versus-strong finding example, what does the phase say is the single difference between the two versions?
<!-- phases: cyber-06-portfolio-projects -->

- [ ] The strong version identifies the root cause, which the weak version leaves implicit
- [ ] The strong version uses more precise technical terminology
- [ ] The strong version reaches a firmer conclusion about whether the activity was malicious
- [x] **In the weak version you are asked to believe the author; in the strong version you are invited to check them**

**Why:** That sentence is the phase's own summary of the seven dimensions it changes, and the seventh is the one the other six serve. The strong finding cites observed process, PID and timestamp, references its evidence paths, names the business impact, separates severity from confidence, and states two limitations. The second option is worth noticing as a trap: the strong version is deliberately **less** certain about intent — "High severity, medium confidence" — because the phase treats a stated confidence level as a professional claim and a bare adjective as an opinion.

### Q9. A learner writes three detection rules that each alert on a variation of the same behaviour. What does the phase say this demonstrates?
<!-- phases: cyber-06-portfolio-projects -->

- [x] **One skill, because three variations of the same detection show the same thing three times**
- [ ] A tuning story, provided at least one rule was refined after firing noisily
- [ ] Three rules, which satisfies the requirement to show range in detection engineering
- [ ] Diligence, because covering variations of one technique is thorough work

**Why:** The phase requires three rules that demonstrate three **different** skills: modifying a built-in rule, writing a custom rule for a specific behaviour, and writing a rule with a tuning story. Its reasoning about the third is the one worth retaining — anyone can make an alert fire, and the skill employers pay for is making alerts fire usefully. The fourth option is a real component of the requirement but describes the third rule's shape rather than answering what three variations of one behaviour demonstrate.

### Q10. Reviewing a project's screenshots, a learner has captioned one with nothing but the bare image path — an empty alt text pointing at `evidence/image1.png`. What does the phase say about this caption, and why?
<!-- phases: cyber-06-portfolio-projects -->

- [ ] It is acceptable in a private repository but not a public one
- [ ] It should be replaced with a longer prose description of what the screenshot shows
- [x] **It is decoration rather than evidence — a caption must say what the image shows, so a reader can trust it without opening it**
- [ ] It is acceptable, because the filename identifies the evidence it contains

**Why:** The phase's contrast is exactly this pair: a caption naming the alert, its identifier, the timestamp and the host is evidence, while a bare path with no description is decoration — the reader cannot judge the image without opening it, so it proves nothing on its own. The reasoning extends to terminal screenshots, which must include the command rather than only its output, because otherwise nobody can tell whether the output came from your lab or a documentation page. The third option is the near-miss: the fix is not more prose but the specific fact that makes the image checkable.

### Q11. A learner's write-up has a smooth narrative with no mistakes in it, and they are considering removing the section describing what went wrong. What does the phase say about that section?
<!-- phases: cyber-06-portfolio-projects -->

- [ ] It should be kept only if the mistake was caused by something outside the learner's control
- [ ] It may be removed if the write-up already includes a Limitations section
- [ ] It should be moved to the Limitations section, where caveats belong
- [x] **Keeping it is not optional — a mistake and its correction is the strongest signal you did the work rather than followed a tutorial**

**Why:** The phase observes that almost nobody includes a mistake and its correction, and that this absence is exactly what makes the inclusion so informative: a tutorial can be transcribed without error, so an errorless account is consistent with not having done the work. This is also why the phase treats the failure part of a write-up as the section that does the most work while being the section most beginners delete. A Limitations section is a different thing — it bounds the conclusion rather than describing the process.

## Domain 3 — Certifications, decided with evidence (5 questions)

### Q12. Phase 7 opens by noting that this is the only phase whose honest answer might be "spend nothing". What is the phase's actual position on certifications?
<!-- phases: cyber-07-certifications -->

- [ ] Certifications are necessary for entry, and the phase recommends buying the highest-value one first
- [ ] Certifications matter only as a tiebreaker between otherwise equal candidates
- [x] **Both failure modes are real — buying a certification as a skills substitute is wrong, and refusing all certifications on principle is also wrong**
- [ ] Certifications are a substitute for skills, and the phase recommends against paying for them

**Why:** The phase states it two-sidedly: it is not "certifications are bad" and it is not "certifications are the answer", but understand what a certification does, what it does not do, and what it is worth to you specifically, then decide with evidence. The first option is the failure mode the lesson opens with — the five-step loop where someone feels stuck, pays, passes, feels credentialed, and finds nothing about their prospects has changed. The second is the opposite failure the phase names in its second part: in sectors that filter on credentials before a human reads the resume, ignoring them entirely means your portfolio is never seen.

### Q13. The phase separates "should I study this material?" from "should I pay for the exam?" Why does it insist these are two decisions?
<!-- phases: cyber-07-certifications -->

- [ ] Because paying for an exam before studying usually results in a failed attempt
- [ ] Because studying without sitting the exam is a waste of the learner's time
- [x] **Because studying is free and the exam is not, so step one can start immediately and step two can wait indefinitely**
- [ ] Because the exam tests practical skill that study cannot develop

**Why:** The phase's reframe is that studying is free and the exam is not, and that learners who blur the two end up neither studying nor earning the certification, because they never start. Keeping them separate lets you do the valuable half immediately and postpone the expensive half without guilt. The fourth option is worth noticing because it inverts the phase's actual claim about what the exam measures: the phase says a certification describes and labs build, so the exam is weak on practical skill rather than being the thing that tests it.

### Q14. A learner completes Phase 7's free-first sequence, closes their gaps, reassesses, and decides not yet to book an exam. How does the phase characterise that outcome?
<!-- phases: cyber-07-certifications -->

- [ ] A deferral, which is acceptable but leaves the phase incomplete
- [ ] A failure of the phase's purpose, since the phase exists to select a certification
- [x] **A successful completion, provided the decision document is filled in — "not yet, and here is why" is a complete answer**
- [ ] A partial completion that should be revisited within ninety days

**Why:** The phase says this twice and without hedging: many learners will reach the reassessment step and reasonably decide to delay, and that is a successful outcome for the phase. Its decision document has five sections, and one of them may be an explicit decision to delay with the reasoning recorded — a decision to delay with those sections filled in is described as a **complete** phase deliverable. This is a position rather than a fact, and it is the one most likely to be answered wrongly by someone who assumes a phase must end in a purchase.

### Q15. In the phase's 30-job-post test, a certification is marked Required in 6 postings, Preferred in 14, and not mentioned in 10. Which category does the phase say actually matters, and why?
<!-- phases: cyber-07-certifications -->

- [ ] Preferred, because it is the largest category and therefore the strongest signal
- [ ] The total of Required and Preferred, because between them they cover two thirds of the market
- [x] **Required, because it is the only category that can actually exclude you**
- [ ] None of them, because a portfolio can substitute in every case

**Why:** The phase states that the pattern which matters is "required", because that is the only category that can exclude you — a preferred certification is a tiebreaker, while a required one is a filter you either pass or do not. Its worked output shows Security+ required in 6 of 30, and the phase's accompanying observation is that the numbers are usually lower than the online discourse suggests, because certification advocacy is loud while postings are quieter and more specific. The grade distinction is the part learners routinely collapse, which is what makes this question worth asking.

### Q16. The phase warns that a certification you cannot afford to maintain becomes a lapsed line on your resume. What property of certifications is this warning about?
<!-- phases: cyber-07-certifications -->

- [ ] That a lapsed certification cannot be re-earned without a full re-examination
- [ ] That certification content expires as the field changes
- [ ] That employers verify certifications at the point of hiring only
- [x] **That a certification is a subscription rather than a purchase, since most require renewal every three years**

**Why:** The phase's framing is that a certification is a subscription, not a purchase, and that the renewal cycle is a cost you are committing to rather than a one-off fee. This matters because it changes the affordability calculation: a certification whose renewal you cannot fund is a line that will need removing later. The fourth option overstates the position — the phase's concern is the recurring cost and the lapsed line, not the mechanics of reinstatement, which it does not describe.

## Domain 4 — Running the search (4 questions)

### Q17. A learner sends 50 applications and receives almost no responses at all. Which part of the process does the phase's funnel say is failing?
<!-- phases: cyber-08-job-application -->

- [x] **Something upstream of the interview — targeting, resume keywords, or the roles being chosen**
- [ ] Nothing identifiable, because a zero-response first pass is the statistically normal outcome
- [ ] The interview, because technical depth is not surviving first contact
- [ ] The learner's qualification level, because 50 applications is beyond the point of diminishing returns

**Why:** The phase's diagnostic point is that the drop happens at the resume screen rather than the interview, and that diagnosing which stage is failing you is the entire value of tracking the search. Almost no responses means the problem is upstream and free to fix; interviews without progression means the interview needs work. The fourth option is a real phase claim used in the wrong place: it says a zero-**offer** first pass is normal and informative, which is a different reading from zero responses, and the phase explicitly contrasts the two.

### Q18. Phase 8 says the resume screen is a filter rather than a judge. What does the phase conclude from a strong candidate being screened out of a slightly wrong role while a weaker candidate matched exactly gets through?
<!-- phases: cyber-08-job-application -->

- [ ] That the weaker candidate will be exposed at the technical interview
- [x] **That it is not fair and not personal either, but a mechanical property of the system that can be worked with**
- [ ] That the resume screen measures qualification accurately and the learner misjudged the role
- [ ] That the process is unfair and the learner should target employers with human review instead

**Why:** The phase's correction is aimed at the beginner reading the funnel as a merit test, which it calls the model that makes the silence unbearable. Reframing the screen as a mechanical filter is what converts an unexplained rejection into information about targeting and keywords. The first option is the tempting over-correction: the phase does not recommend avoiding the screen, it recommends working with its mechanics — mirroring the posting's vocabulary honestly, keeping a single-column layout, and choosing roles that match.

### Q19. A learner's applications are ambiguous about whether their security work was professional or self-directed. What does the phase say this framing trades away?
<!-- phases: cyber-08-job-application -->

- [ ] Nothing, provided the learner can answer questions about the work convincingly
- [ ] A small interview advantage in exchange for a large resume-screen advantage
- [x] **A small resume-screen advantage in exchange for a large interview advantage, which is where the decision is actually made**
- [ ] Nothing, because employers assume entry-level security work is self-directed

**Why:** The phase's trade is stated exactly in that direction, and the mechanism is the follow-up question: if the resume implies professional experience, the interviewer asks about escalation paths, service levels, or a change board, and you must either admit the framing or invent an answer. The honest version — a labelled self-directed section with the lab context stated once — produces the hireable conclusion that this person has no professional experience but is capable and honest. The first option is the trap: the phase's point is that ambiguity survives the resume screen and fails the interview, where the decision is made.

### Q20. The phase summarises its whole strategy for converting a cold market into a warm one in a single sentence. What is it, and what does the phase say the order is?
<!-- phases: cyber-08-job-application -->

- [ ] Apply widely, then follow up relentlessly — volume produces the response rate
- [ ] Get the certification first, then apply — the credential is what opens the conversation
- [x] **Maximise human engagement, then be ready for it — ask for information first, and ask for a referral only after a genuine connection**
- [ ] Ask for the referral immediately, then build rapport once you are talking

**Why:** The phase states it as one sentence — maximise human engagement, then be ready for it — and calls that the strategy for the whole phase. The order inside it is the part people invert: the referral sequence asks for information first and a referral later, and the phase's condition is that referrals beat cold applications **but only after a genuine connection**. The fourth option is the inversion the phase warns against, and the first is the volume strategy the phase's own tracker diagnosis contradicts, since it treats applications as diagnostic rather than as a quota. The phase's other summary line — schedule rest deliberately, because this phase has delayed feedback and no progress bar — is there because this is the phase where burn-out-prone learners quit.

## What a score does and does not mean

There is no pass mark here, and no number on this page implies readiness for a SOC, GRC, or any other role. **The questions are written to the reasoning these phases teach, not to a certification blueprint**, because none of these four phases is aligned to a certification.

A useful reading of a miss is narrower than "I got this wrong." Most of these questions turn on a judgement you make about your own situation: whether you counted or trusted a reputation, whether you treated a constraint as a veto or averaged it, whether you could name the boundary of your own evidence, whether you read silence as information or as a verdict. If you missed several in one domain, the likely gap is a habit rather than a fact, and the mapped phase is where to practise it. Phase 8's own exit criterion makes this point better than any score can: the phase is complete when you can explain your transition, walk through a project with evidence, and answer basic technical questions calmly — **not when you have an offer**.
