# Phase 9 — Job Application Plan

## Goal of this phase

Apply consistently to remote entry-level IT roles while improving your resume and interview performance from feedback.

## Estimated time

**8–12 weeks alongside study**. Some people land faster, but beginners without a degree should plan for a longer search.

## Skills you'll gain

- Search for realistic entry-level roles.
- Customize resume keywords without lying.
- Track applications and follow-ups.
- Prepare for screening calls and technical interviews.
- Avoid scams and low-quality job traps.

## Target roles

Prioritize:

- Helpdesk Technician
- IT Support Specialist
- Service Desk Analyst
- Technical Support Representative with IT products
- Desktop Support, remote/hybrid if possible
- NOC Technician trainee/junior
- Junior System Administrator assistant
- SaaS Technical Support Specialist

Avoid as first targets unless clearly junior:

- Security Engineer
- Penetration Tester
- Cloud Security Engineer

## Lesson: Job Application Plan

### Why this lesson exists

Everything in Phases 1 to 8 was preparation. This phase is the search itself, and it is where most self-taught beginners fail — not because their skills are insufficient, but because their **process** is. They apply to twelve jobs over three months, receive silence, and conclude that the industry will not hire without a degree. The real cause is almost always volume, targeting, or tracking.

This phase fixes the process with three disciplines:

1. **Volume with consistency.** Applications per week, not applications per mood.
2. **Tracking.** A spreadsheet that tells you what is working and what is not.
3. **Iteration.** Adjust based on results, every two weeks, like a test loop.

**Time to complete:** 8–12 weeks alongside study, and there is no way to compress it. The search is not something you finish before applying — it runs in parallel with the remaining study, and the phase note is honest: beginners without a degree should plan for a longer search. Plan for the longer timeline emotionally, and you will not interpret a quiet week as a verdict on your worth.

**The mindset that makes this survivable.** Treat the job search as a technical problem with a feedback loop, because that is exactly what it is. Applications submitted is your sample size. Interviews are positive signal. Silence is data about your resume's top third, not about you as a person. Every rejection tells you something specific you can fix, and that is genuinely good news in a process that otherwise feels arbitrary.

### Part 1 — Targeting: applying where you can actually win

#### The realistic entry-level roles

The phase lists these as priorities, and the list is well chosen because each one is a genuine door into IT:

| Role | What it really is | Why it is a good entry point |
|---|---|---|
| **Helpdesk Technician** | Phone, chat, and ticket-based support | Core support experience; the standard first rung |
| **IT Support Specialist** | Broad internal support, often at one company | Variety: hardware, software, accounts |
| **Service Desk Analyst** | Structured ticket queue, often with SLAs | Teaches process discipline and prioritisation |
| **Technical Support Rep (IT products)** | Supporting a software product for customers | Product depth; often remote-friendly |
| **Desktop Support** | Hands-on endpoints, imaging, deployments | Strong hardware and endpoint skills |
| **NOC Technician (trainee/junior)** | Monitoring alerts, first-line network response | Uses the Phase 6 monitoring skills directly |
| **Junior Sysadmin Assistant** | Supporting an admin team | Fastest path upward if you can get it |
| **SaaS Technical Support** | Supporting a cloud product | Growing field; usually remote and English-heavy |

**Read job ads for the *actual* seniority, not the title.** Titles are inconsistent across companies: one company's "IT Support Specialist" is another's junior sysadmin. What matters is the requirements section. If it asks for two years of experience, apply anyway if your skills match — but do not make it your main effort. If it asks for Windows, basic networking, ticketing, and good communication, that is your role, whatever it is called.

#### What to avoid as a first target, and why

The phase lists Security Engineer, Penetration Tester, Cloud Security Engineer, Senior Sysadmin, and DevOps Engineer as roles to avoid as first targets. The reason is not that you could never do them. It is that the gap between "entry-level candidate" and those requirements is large enough that applications there mostly produce rejections, and rejection volume without interviews is how people lose momentum.

**Every application has a cost** — time, morale, and the opportunity to apply somewhere realistic instead. Spending it where you can plausibly win is strategy, not defeatism.

That said, the skills you are building are exactly the foundations for those roles later. The networking from Phase 3, the sysadmin work from Phase 5, the security awareness throughout — they are the substrate. The path is real; it just starts one or two rungs lower. **The fastest way to become a security engineer in three years is to get an IT support job this year.**

#### Where to look in the Philippines, and how remote changes the picture

The phase lists PH-friendly boards, and they serve different purposes:

- **OnlineJobs.ph** — the main marketplace for remote work with overseas employers, especially US, Australian, and UK clients. Individual employer profiles let you see who you are dealing with, and your profile does the initial selling.
- **Kalibrr** and **JobStreet PH** — mainstream PH employment, including hybrid and on-site roles at local companies and BPOs.
- **LinkedIn Jobs** — where most international remote postings appear, and where your Phase 8 profile starts working for you.
- **Indeed PH** — broad aggregator, useful for volume.
- **RemoteOK** and **We Work Remotely** — international remote boards. Competition is higher, but remote-first employers there are often more comfortable hiring from outside their own country than traditional companies are.

**The time-zone advantage nobody talks about.** Philippine working hours overlap substantially with Australia, Japan, Singapore, and the US west coast. When you mention availability across those time zones, you are offering something a domestic candidate cannot. **Say it explicitly in your cover note.** It is a concrete advantage of hiring you rather than an abstract geographic fact.

### Part 2 — Volume, customisation, and tracking

#### The numbers, and what they mean

The phase targets **5–10 applications per week initially, rising to 15 per week** once your resume is stable, with a running total of at least 50. That is not arbitrary. It reflects the arithmetic of entry-level hiring: response rates for a self-taught candidate with no formal experience are low, often a few percent for cold applications. Fifty applications at a 4% interview rate is two interviews. A hundred is four. **Volume is not a substitute for quality; it is what makes quality visible.**

Two things to internalise about the numbers:

- **Consistency beats intensity.** Five applications every week for twelve weeks (60 total) outperforms thirty applications in one enthusiastic weekend followed by three weeks of nothing. Recruiters contact candidates when postings are live, and a steady pipeline means you are always in the market.
- **Track the conversion, not just the count.** Applications → responses → interviews → offers. If you get responses but no interviews, the problem is your resume's depth. If interviews but no offers, the problem is interview performance. If applications but no responses, the problem is your top third or your targeting. **Each stage's failure has a specific fix**, and the tracker is how you find out which stage you are failing at.

#### Customising the top third without rewriting the resume

The phase task is to customise the top third of your resume for each role type, and that is the correct scope. Rewriting a whole resume per application is unsustainable at fifteen applications a week and will burn you out in two weeks.

The top third is the summary, the skills list, and the first project bullet. Those are the parts a scanner reads first. Customising means:

1. **Read the job ad and list its recurring nouns** — the specific technologies and responsibilities it names.
2. **Match the ones you honestly have** into your summary and skills, using the ad's own words.
3. **Reorder your project bullets** so the most relevant project appears first. Same projects, different order, different emphasis.
4. **Adjust the summary's first line** to name the role type you are applying for.

That is a ten-minute job per application, and it retains the scan-detection logic: the reader sees their own vocabulary in the top third and keeps reading.

#### Two resume versions, and why two is the right number

The deliverable asks for two: **Helpdesk/IT Support** and **NOC/Networking Support**. Two versions work because they correspond to two genuinely different role families, and each can foreground a different set of your projects:

- **Helpdesk/IT Support version** — leads with troubleshooting write-ups, ticketing samples, the asset inventory, and communication skills. Emphasises the human and process side.
- **NOC/Networking version** — leads with the lab network, subnetting, packet captures, monitoring runbooks, and alert response. Emphasises the technical and analytical side.

Maintain more than two and you will start confusing them, and quality will slip. Two versions, each customised in the top third per application, is the sustainable structure.

#### Following up professionally

The checklist includes professional follow-up, and it is worth doing properly because most candidates do not.

The etiquette, briefly:

- **After applying, wait five to seven business days** before any follow-up. Faster than that reads as impatient.
- **One follow-up, politely worded** — restate interest, briefly restate the match, ask whether there is an update on timelines. Do not chase a second time.
- **After an interview, send a short thank-you within 24 hours.** Reference something specific discussed, and confirm continued interest. Three sentences is enough.
- **If rejected, ask for feedback once.** Most employers will not answer, and some will. The ones who do are worth listening to carefully — candid feedback from a real hiring process is more valuable than any career article.
- **Keep every interaction in the tracker.** Who you spoke to, when, and what was said. When they call back in six weeks, being the candidate who remembers the conversation is an advantage.

**Never follow up by phone unless they invited it**, and never follow up more than once. Persistent is not the same as professional, and the difference is entirely in the frequency.

### Part 3 — Interview preparation

#### The five questions every interview will contain

The phase lists five answers to prepare, and they are exactly the right five, because between them they cover nearly every opening exchange:

1. **"Tell me about yourself."** — Ninety seconds: where you are, what you have been learning, what you are looking for. This is your Phase 7 self-direction story with the portfolio attached. Structure it as present → background → direction.
2. **"Why IT?"** — Have a real answer. The weak version is "I like computers". The strong version names a specific thing you enjoy about the work — diagnosing, or the moment a frustrated user becomes calm, or the satisfaction of automating something tedious.
3. **"You don't have a degree — how did you learn?"** — The Phase 7 answer, delivered without defensiveness, followed by a portfolio link or an offer to walk through an artefact.
4. **"Tell me about a difficult user."** — Your Phase 7 STAR story. The good version shows you found the real problem behind the complaint.
5. **"Give me a troubleshooting example."** — Your best technical story, told in full: symptom, what you ruled out, what you tried that failed, what worked, and what you learned. **The failed attempts are the most persuasive part**, because they demonstrate method rather than luck.

Rehearse these out loud, timed, with the follow-up questions included. Every one of them will be probed.

#### The twenty technical questions

The phase asks for twenty technical Q&A covering DNS, DHCP, IP, VPN, password reset, MFA, Windows logs, and Linux permissions. The list is deliberately foundational, and that is correct — entry-level interviews test fundamentals, not depth.

| Topic | The question behind the question |
|---|---|
| DNS | Do you know what translates names to addresses, and how you would test it? |
| DHCP | Do you know how a device gets an address, and what failure looks like? |
| IP addressing and subnetting | Can you reason about whether two hosts can talk? |
| VPN | Do you understand what it is for, and what "VPN is down" actually breaks? |
| Password reset | Do you follow identity-verification procedure, or just reset on request? |
| MFA | Do you know what it protects against, and how you would troubleshoot enrolment? |
| Windows logs | Can you find evidence of what happened on a machine? |
| Linux permissions | Do you understand `rwx`, ownership, and what a permission error means? |

**Answer in three layers.** For each question, be ready to give: (1) a one-sentence plain explanation, (2) a real-world example from your lab, and (3) how you would check or troubleshoot it. The third layer is what separates a candidate who has read about DNS from one who has watched a query fail.

**"I don't know, but here is how I would find out"** is a complete and acceptable answer to anything outside your depth. It is also more honest than a bluff, and interviewers recognise it as professional.

#### The trap: scams and unrealistic offers

The checklist includes watching for scams, and this deserves detail because entry-level remote job seekers in the Philippines are actively targeted.

**The universal red flag: any request for money from you.** Legitimate employers never charge for training, equipment, onboarding, background checks, software, or "processing". Not as a deposit, not refundable, not as a test. **If money is requested, it is a scam, and the conversation ends there.**

Other warning signs:

- **An offer without a real interview.** A job offer after a chat message and no conversation about your skills is not a job offer.
- **Payment in gift cards or crypto, or via personal accounts** — and requests for your bank details before any formal agreement.
- **Vague employer identity.** No company website, no verifiable LinkedIn presence, no address, and a hiring manager whose profile was created last month.
- **A rate wildly above market for the stated skill level.** Unrealistic pay is bait, and the eventual ask is always money or identity documents.
- **Requests for identity documents before an offer**, or for more than is normal for onboarding. A scan of your passport "to verify" a job you have not been offered is a red flag.
- **"Equipment reimbursement" schemes**, where you buy gear with your own money and are promised repayment. You will not be repaid.
- **Urgency and pressure.** "This offer expires in two hours" is a manipulation tactic, not a business practice. Real employers give you time.
- **A recruiter who moves the conversation immediately off-platform** to a personal chat app, away from the job board's protections.

**Do this before accepting anything:** search the company name plus the word "scam", check how old the domain is, look up the hiring manager on LinkedIn and see whether they have a history, and ask for a video call with the team before signing anything. A real employer will accommodate that easily. A scammer will make excuses.

**If it seems too good to be true, it is.** That instinct is correct, and this phase explicitly teaches you to trust it.

### Part 4 — Running the search for twelve weeks

#### The tracker, and the columns that matter

The phase specifies the columns, and there is a reason each one exists:

| Column | Why it is there |
|---|---|
| Company | So you can recognise the name when they call |
| Role | So you know which version of your resume you sent |
| Link | To re-read the ad before an interview |
| Date applied | So you know when follow-up is due |
| Resume version | To correlate outcomes with the version you used |
| Status | Applied → screening → interview → offer / rejected |
| Follow-up | Your next action date — the column that keeps you honest |
| Notes | The contact's name, what was discussed, what to remember |

**The Follow-up column is the operational core.** Everything else is a record; that column is what tells you what to do today. Open the tracker each morning, sort by follow-up date, and do the day's work. That is the entire system.

**Also log the rejections, and why.** If eight rejections cite "requires degree" and three cite "requires experience", you have learned something concrete about your market. That is a study plan, not a verdict.

#### The two-week review loop

The phase asks you to review failed applications every two weeks and adjust keywords. That cycle is deliberately short, because the point is to find out what is not working before you have spent eight weeks doing it.

At each review, ask three questions:

1. **What was the response rate?** If it is near zero after twenty-plus applications, the top third of your resume is not matching. Re-read the ads you applied to and compare their vocabulary with yours, literally side by side.
2. **Where did applications stop progressing?** Screening calls that go nowhere usually mean your answers to "tell me about yourself" or the degree question need work. Interviews that go nowhere usually mean the technical or behavioural stories are weak.
3. **What did you learn that you do not yet know?** Every ad naming a tool you have never touched is a study list. Add it, learn it at a basic level, and write a short portfolio note. This is how the search feeds back into the curriculum.

**Adjust one variable at a time**, so you know what caused the change. Rewriting the resume, the LinkedIn profile, and the cover note template simultaneously will move your numbers, but you will not know why.

#### The cover note that actually gets read

Three paragraphs are enough, and most employers will read them briefly:

1. **What you are applying for, and one line of genuine fit** — referencing something specific from the ad, not a generic statement about the industry.
2. **What you have built** — one or two specific artefacts with a portfolio link. "I've built a PowerShell inventory script and a set of helpdesk ticket templates; they're in my portfolio at [link]."
3. **Availability and a clear close** — time-zone overlap, start date, and willingness to do a technical exercise or a call.

**Put the portfolio link in the first paragraph.** It is your single strongest differentiator, and burying it in the third paragraph costs you the click.

#### Contacting people, not just applying to postings

The phase asks for **five polite LinkedIn connection requests per week** to IT recruiters or support leads. This is not busywork, and it is the part of the search most candidates skip.

Why it works: most postings attract far more applicants than the hiring manager can read carefully, and a human recommendation moves you out of that pile. A short, specific message does that without being pushy:

> "Hi [Name] — I'm an entry-level IT support candidate based in the Philippines, remote-ready and available across your time zone. I've been building a portfolio of hands-on labs: a PowerShell inventory script, a set of helpdesk ticket templates, and a routed lab network with packet captures. If you ever have a junior support opening, I'd be glad to be considered. Portfolio: [link]. No need to reply if it's not relevant."

What makes that message work:

- **It is short.** Four sentences.
- **It says what you want** — junior support openings — so the reader can act on it instantly.
- **It gives evidence, not adjectives.** A link, not a claim.
- **It gives the reader an easy exit**, which paradoxically increases replies, because nobody feels cornered.
- **It is sent once.** No follow-up unless they respond.

Send five a week, keep a note of who you contacted in the tracker, and do not be discouraged by a low reply rate. **One useful conversation is worth fifty cold applications**, and you only need to find that one.

#### What the twelve weeks are actually for

It is worth being clear about what this phase builds, because the timeline can feel discouraging.

Those weeks are not a waiting period. They are when you:

- **Learn what the market actually asks for**, in real language, from real ads.
- **Fix the resume iteratively** based on evidence rather than guesswork.
- **Rehearse interviews until the stories are owned** rather than memorised.
- **Keep studying**, because every ad naming an unfamiliar tool is a lesson plan.
- **Build the habit of looking**, which is a skill you will use for the rest of your career.

**Most people fail this phase by stopping, not by lacking skill.** The search rewards persistence more than it rewards perfection, and the candidate still applying in week ten with a tracker full of notes is in a completely different position from the one who sent ten applications and drifted away.

Keep the process running. It works.

### Key takeaways

- **The search is a process to run, not a lottery to enter.** Volume, tracking, and iteration are the whole job.
- **Target roles where entry-level is actually the requirement.** Titles lie; the requirements section does not.
- **5–10 applications per week, rising to 15**, and 50+ total — consistency beats intensity.
- **Track conversion, not just count.** Applications → responses → interviews → offers tells you which stage to fix.
- **Customise the top third** with the ad's own vocabulary, honestly. Ten minutes per application.
- **Two resume versions**: Helpdesk/IT Support and NOC/Networking Support.
- **One polite follow-up, then stop.** Thank-you within 24 hours of an interview; ask for feedback once after a rejection.
- **Answer technical questions in three layers:** plain explanation, lab example, troubleshooting approach.
- **"I don't know, but here's how I'd find out"** is a valid, professional answer.
- **Any request for money is a scam.** No exceptions, ever.
- **Message real people, not just postings.** One conversation is worth fifty cold applications.
- **Review every two weeks.** Adjust one variable at a time, and let the ads tell you what to study next.

### Practice this next

The tasks below are the phase, and the order matters. Build the tracker first — with the Follow-up column working — because everything else depends on it. Then create or update the four profiles (OnlineJobs.ph, LinkedIn, JobStreet, Kalibrr), write both resume versions, and start applying at the low end of the volume target while your resume is still settling. Prepare the twenty technical Q&A answers in three layers each, and think about the specific reason each question is on the list. The LinkedIn connection requests are a genuine part of the strategy, not filler: a message to a support lead that mentions a specific thing you built is a real conversation starter, and some of your interviews will come from exactly that. Finally, do the two-week review honestly, because the search that iterates beats the search that merely repeats.

## Free vs Paid

### What's free and enough

All listed job boards are free for applicants. Google Sheets and Google Calendar are enough.

### What's paid and why you'd upgrade

LinkedIn Premium can show extra applicant insights, but it does not replace skills, portfolio, or volume.

### When it's worth paying

Do not pay while budget is $0. If you later have income, consider one month only during heavy job search, but it is optional.
